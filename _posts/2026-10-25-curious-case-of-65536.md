## An Unusual Property of a Power of Two

$$
65536 = 2^{16}
$$

There's nothing particularly surprising about this number at first glance. It is a power of two, a familiar boundary in computing, and a number that appears in plenty of engineering applications.

But looks at its decimal representation:

$$ 
65536 
$$

Now consider the single-digit powers of two:

$$
1,\ 2,\ 4,\ 8
$$

None of these digits appears anywhere in $65536$.

This raises a curious question: is $65536$ the only power of two whose decimal represents avoids all four of these digits?

## The Conjecture

Let us call a power of two **digit-avoiding* if its decimal representation contains none of the digits $1,2,4,8$.

Our observation gives us one example:

$$
2^{16}=65536
$$

The conjecture is that this is the only one.

$$
\boxed{
2^n \text{ avoids } 1,2,4,8
\iff n=16
}
$$

At first, this might seem like something we could verify by simply checking a few powers of two. However, there is a difference between finding a unique example in a finite search and proving that no other example exists.

Before tackling that question, we can establish a few restrictions on what a possible solution must look like. 

## Looking at the Last Digit

The last digit of successive powers of two follows a repeating pattern:

$$
2,\ 4,\ 8,\ 6,\ 2,\ 4,\ 8,\ 6,\ldots
$$

Since $2,4,8$ are forbidden, the only possible last digit is $6$.

Consequently, every positive exponent satisfying our condition must be divisible by four:

$$
\boxed{n\equiv0\pmod4}
$$

This means that we do not need to check every power of two. We can immediately eliminate three out of every four positive exponents.
Even more interestingly, every possible solution must itself be a power of $16$:

$$
2^{4k} = 16^k
$$

## Looking at the Last Two Digits

Going one step further

For powers of $16$, the last two decimal digits follow a cycle:

$$
16,\ 56,\ 96,\ 36,\ 76,\ 16,\ldots
$$

The cycle repeats every five powers of $16$.

Notice that the first possibility, $16$, containes the forbidden digit $1$. Therefore any valid exponent must also satisfy

$$
\boxed{n\not\equiv4\pmod{20}}
$$

Combining our two observations, a valid exponent must satisfy

$$
n\equiv0\pmod4,
\qquad
n\not\equiv4\pmod{20}.
$$

We have eleminated even more candidates without examining their complete decimal represenations.

Ofcourse, this still does not prove that $65536$ is unique. A power of two could pass both tests and contain a forbidden digit somewhere further to the left.

## Searching for Other Examples

We can investigate the conjecture computationally with a simple Python script:

```python
for n in range(100_001):
    number = str(2**n)

    if not any(digit in number for digit in "1248"):
        print(n,number)
```

The program checks every decimal digit of each power of two within the specified range. Any exponent that satisfies the condition is printed alongside its corresponding number.

In a reported search extending to $n=10^8$, the only candidate found was

$$
2^{16}=65536.
$$

This is compelling computational evidence, but it is important to understand what it establishes. A search can verify every exponent within its range; it cannot rule out a solution beyond that range.

## Where Does This Leave Us?

We began with a simple observation about a number that happens to avoid four particular decimal digits.

Along the way, we established that any positive solution must be a power of $16$ and must satisfy additional congruence restrictions. computational searches have also found no other example within their tested ranges.

Yet the central question remains:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Is }65536\text{ the only such power of two?}
}
$$

The difficulty is that the binary structure of $2^n$ is simple, but its decimal digits do not follow an equally simple pattern. Controlling the last few digits is relatively straightforward; controlling every digit, for every exponent, is another matter entirely.

For now, the unqiueness of $65536$ remains a conjecture in this investigation rather than proven theorem. 

Sometimes, a number as ordinary-looking as $65536$ is enough to lead us into a surprisingly difficult mathematical problem.
